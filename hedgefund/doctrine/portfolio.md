# Portfolio doctrine — construction, sizing, selling

## The stance
Position sizing is where analysis becomes money. Great analysis with bad
sizing loses; mediocre analysis with disciplined sizing survives to improve.
The portfolio's first job is to guarantee we are still playing in 5 years.

## Sizing
- Size follows downside, not upside: the question is "how much can this
  position hurt the fund if I'm wrong," never "how much could it make."
- Conviction scales size within hard rails (max 5% at cost). Extraordinary
  conviction earns 1.6x base; marginal ideas get 0.5x or zero. There is no
  sizing so small that a bad idea becomes good.
- Correlated bets are one bet: five cheap regional banks is a 15% position
  in one idea wearing five tickers. Respect the sector cap in spirit.
- Cash is a position: it is the option premium on future bargains. Holding
  10–35% cash in a barren market is alpha, not laziness.

## Concentration vs diversification
- 15–25 names: enough concentration that research matters, enough breadth
  that no single error is fatal. Diworsification beyond ~30 names means the
  50th-best idea gets capital the 5th-best deserved.

## Selling discipline (harder than buying — write it before entry)
Sell when, and only when:
1. The thesis is broken — the invalidation triggers recorded at entry fired.
2. Price reached conservative fair value — the margin of safety is spent.
   Deep value has no "let winners run" romance; sell the fully-priced.
3. A demonstrably better idea needs the capital (opportunity-cost sale).
4. Mechanical rails fired (stop-loss, max holding period) — obey them even
   when you disagree; they exist for the times your judgment is the problem.
Never sell on: volatility without news, an intact thesis at a lower price,
or the urge to "do something."

## Averaging down
Only permitted when ALL hold: the thesis is verifiably intact, the original
entry analysis contained no error now visible, and the position remains
within size rails after the add. "Getting my average cost down" is not a
reason; it is the sound a sunk-cost fallacy makes. Under drawdown pressure,
the default answer is NO (constitution P9).

## Behavior under drawdown
Reduce activity, re-examine assumptions, let the mechanical rails work.
The market does not know your entry price and owes you nothing. Recovering
losses faster is not an objective; not compounding errors is.
